## Life & Annuity eApplications and the Future of New Business Intake

### AI is reshaping expectations before submission even starts.

**1. Introduction**

Increasingly, the application process itself is expected to behave like an intelligent system guiding users, anticipating needs, and adapting in real time. The shift is not just toward digitization, but toward decision-aware, AI-assisted intake experiences.

The U.S. life and annuity market is producing at record levels, with more than 4 million Americans turning 65 each year through 2029, and a larger proportion of them retiring without a pension than any prior generation. Carriers have responded with a wave of product innovation that has broadened the market considerably. Retail annuity sales topped $460 billion in 2025, the fourth consecutive year of record production, and individual life premiums have followed a similar trajectory. The market is not slowing down.

That premium volume is flowing through workflows that were not built for it. Manual processing, paper-dependent intake, and fragmented post-submission workflows have not kept pace with what carriers are now writing, and the products driving growth tend to be among the most application-intensive in the portfolio.

At the same time, producers and policyholders are arriving with different expectations than they had 2-3 years ago. AI has become part of daily life, in the banking apps they use, the financial platforms they work on, and the tools they run their businesses with. Full AI adoption across insurer value chains jumped from 8% to 34% in a single year. Producers increasingly expect the same intelligent, responsive experience from a carrier's application workflow that they get everywhere else.

For life and annuity carriers managing record production volumes, increasingly complex products, and a producer base whose expectations have shifted, the new business operation is under pressure from every direction.

**2. Distribution Realities in Life and Annuity**

Life and annuity insurance is, at its core, a relationship business. Consumers don't buy life insurance or an annuity the same way they buy a plane ticket or open a savings account. They buy it through producers they trust, agents and advisors who understand their financial situation, their family circumstances, and their long-term goals.

A fully underwritten application is not a short form-fill. It almost always requires a structured conversation, about health history, finances, beneficiaries, and coverage needs, that requires a skilled advisor to guide a prospective client through, often taking weeks or months. That process builds the kind of trust that drives placement, stickiness, and referrals.

Captive agents operate exclusively within a single carrier's product portfolio and under that carrier's systems, training, and compliance requirements. Independent agents place business across multiple carriers and owe allegiance to none of them in particular. Independent marketing organizations distribute primarily annuity products through networks of independent producers who often have deep product expertise. In direct-to-consumer channels, AI-driven reflexive questioning is particularly critical, replacing the guidance traditionally provided by an agent.

**3. The eApp Evolution: From Paper and PDFs to Intelligent AI**

The first generation of electronic applications moved paper forms onto screens, letting agents complete applications digitally, capture e-signatures, and submit business electronically. But it was fundamentally a digitization exercise, not a process transformation.

The second generation introduced rules logic at the point of submission: reflexive questions that appeared or disappeared based on prior answers, real-time field validation, and product-specific form logic that reduced not-in-good-order applications. Rules-based validation is only as good as the rules that are written. It catches known errors in known fields. It does not reason about incomplete information or learn from outcomes.

The third generation, where the industry is today, embeds intelligence across the entire application and intake process, from initial data capture through requirements fulfilment and straight-through processing decisioning. Post-submission, intelligent workflows powered by AI agents can manage requirements follow-up, route cases based on complexity, surface medical record summaries for underwriting review, and communicate status in real time across the distribution channel.

**4. Conversational vs Form-based**

Traditional eApplications are form-driven. AI-native eApplications are conversation-driven. Forms ask predefined questions; AI asks contextual questions. Forms validate fields; AI understands intent and risk. Forms capture data; AI improves decision readiness.

**5. AI's Role Across the New Business Lifecycle**

Industry benchmarking consistently identifies cost per application, cycle time, and underwriter productivity as the metrics that most clearly separate carriers investing in automation from those that are not. Carriers running AI-assisted new business processes issue policies in roughly a third of the time it takes their manual counterparts, at dramatically lower cost per application.

Ease of doing business with a particular carrier consistently ranks as a top concern among distributors. When a case gets stuck in manual review, or a requirements letter goes out late, or an agent has to call in for a status update, the cost shows up in both operations and relationships.

**6. Considerations for Life and Annuity Insurance Carriers**

The most useful starting point is an honest assessment of where friction is highest and most costly in your specific new business lifecycle. Platform value is only realized when AI capabilities connect and integrate with existing illustration systems, distribution portals, policy administration systems, and underwriting infrastructure.

Distribution channel composition shapes platform requirements in ways that are easy to underestimate. A carrier writing primarily through captive agents has different requirements than one distributing heavily through broker-general agencies, independent marketing organizations, or a mix of all four.

Change management is where many technology implementations fall short of their potential. The platform is rarely the limiting factor; adoption across field offices, distribution partners, and internal operations teams is. Carriers that have achieved meaningful improvements in new business efficiency have typically done so by investing as heavily in training, workflow redesign, and distributor enablement as in the technology itself.

The future of new business intake is not digital forms, it is intelligent, adaptive conversations powered by AI.
